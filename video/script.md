# Video script: "Speculative Decoding: Does the Speedup Survive Production?"

Target length: 15–17 minutes at a relaxed speaking pace (~150 words per minute, ~2,500 words).
Each "## Slide N" block is the narration for slide N of `slides/speculative-decoding-deck.pdf`.
The same text is embedded as speaker notes in the PPTX, and `scripts/make_video.sh` uses it for the narrated draft video.

## Slide 1 — Title

Hi everyone, and welcome. In this short story I'm going to talk about speculative decoding, one of the most popular tricks for making large language models generate text faster. The question I want to answer is simple: does the speedup actually survive when you move from a research demo to a real production server? To answer it, I'll walk through four papers released in 2026: two surveys and two careful benchmark studies. Together they give us a map of the field, a reality check, and a few surprises. At the end I'll share a practical cheat-sheet and my own opinions.

## Slide 2 — The question

Let's start with why this matters. Speculative decoding promises something rare in machine learning systems: a free speedup, often quoted as two to three times, with no change to the model's output. The idea is that a small drafter guesses a few tokens ahead, and the big model checks all of them in a single pass. If the guesses are good, you get several tokens for the price of one. The catch is that most of those headline numbers come from research prototypes running one request at a time. Real servers don't work that way. They batch sixty-four or a hundred and twenty-eight requests together, they use paged KV caches, and they serve images and long reasoning traces. So the agenda for today is: a quick refresher, a unified taxonomy, the production reality check, two frontier directions, multimodal drafters and relaxed verification, the serving layer underneath, and finally what I would actually deploy.

## Slide 3 — Four papers

Here are the four papers. The anchor is "Speculative Decoding: Performance or Illusion?" from UC Berkeley, by Xiaoxuan Liu, Jiaxiang Yu, Jongseok Park, Ion Stoica and Alvin Cheung. It's the first systematic study of speculative decoding inside vLLM, a widely deployed inference engine. The second is a survey and empirical diagnosis of multimodal speculative decoding from Nanjing University and China Unicom, which introduces a new taxonomy of drafters. The third is a practical investigation of training-free relaxed speculative decoding from Imperial College London and Graphcore. It asks what happens if we stop insisting on exactly lossless output. And the fourth is a survey on system-aware KV cache optimization from the University of Melbourne, published in ACL Findings 2026, which describes the serving layer that every speedup depends on.

## Slide 4 — How speculative decoding works

A quick refresher. In normal decoding, the large model produces one token per forward pass, so five tokens means five sequential passes. On a modern GPU each of those passes is dominated by reading the model's weights from memory, and the arithmetic units sit mostly idle. Speculative decoding uses that idle compute. A cheap drafter proposes, say, five tokens. The drafter could be a small model, a light head attached to the big model, or even a simple text lookup. The target model then scores all five positions in one forward pass. A rejection-sampling rule keeps the longest prefix of guesses that agrees with the target, and adds one bonus token from the target itself. In this example, three guesses survive, plus the bonus, so we get four tokens from one big-model pass. And because of how the acceptance rule is built, the output distribution is exactly the same as normal decoding. That's what "lossless" means.

## Slide 5 — The speedup formula

There's one equation worth remembering. The Berkeley paper writes the expected speedup in terms of three quantities: alpha, the probability that each guess is accepted; k, the number of tokens you draft per step; and c, the cost of the drafter relative to one pass of the target. I plotted it here. On the left, with a cheap drafter and high acceptance, drafting longer keeps paying off, and on paper you can reach more than four times. On the right, the drafter costs about thirty-seven percent of a target pass, which is roughly what the Berkeley team measured for a small draft model serving an 8B target. Now a moderately good drafter barely breaks even, and if you draft too far you become slower than not speculating at all. Keep this picture in mind, because every result in the rest of the talk is really a story about alpha, k and c shifting when real workloads and real servers get involved.

## Slide 6 — One map of the field

Each paper brings its own taxonomy, and at first they look unrelated. But I think they're really four independent design choices that every speculative decoding system has to make. Column one: who drafts? That ranges from training-free n-gram lookup, through sequential drafters like EAGLE, to multi-position heads like MTP, to block-parallel diffusion drafters. Column two: what shape are the guesses? A single chain, a tree of alternatives, an adaptive length, or routing between methods. Column three: how strict is the check? Either strictly lossless, or one of several relaxed rules. And column four: what serves it? The KV-cache scheduling, placement and storage underneath, plus the engine and batch size. The important point is that the columns interact. A choice that's great in one column can be terrible depending on another, and the papers show exactly that.

## Slide 7 — The L0, L1, L2 ladder

Let me zoom into column one, using the multimodal survey's vocabulary, which I found really clarifying. It classifies drafters by how much parallelism happens on the drafter side. Level zero is sequential drafting: one drafter step gives one token. Small draft models and the EAGLE family live here. They're accurate because each guess sees the previous guesses, but drafting k tokens takes k serial steps. Level one is multi-position drafting: several heads predict the next, the one after, and so on, all from the same hidden state in one shot. Medusa and the MTP heads that ship with models like DeepSeek-V3 and GLM-4.5 are examples. They're cheap, but the later positions are guessed blind. Level two is block-parallel drafting: the drafter generates or refines a whole block jointly, usually with a small diffusion model. DFlash and DSpark are the examples, and as we'll see, they currently lead the leaderboard. Outside this ladder sits n-gram lookup, which simply copies what followed the current text earlier in the context. It needs no training and no GPU.

## Slide 8 — The vLLM study setup

Now to the reality check. The Berkeley team ran every major variant inside vLLM with all the production optimizations on, including CUDA graphs and chunked prefill. They used H100 GPUs, with four-way tensor parallelism for the seventy-billion and hundred-and-six-billion parameter models. The models were Llama 3.1 8B, Llama 3 70B, Qwen3 8B in normal and thinking modes, and GLM-4.5-Air. The methods were n-gram, EAGLE, EAGLE-3, separate draft models and MTP, with tree verification tested separately in SGLang. The workloads covered summarization, chat, code editing, math, and hard reasoning, at batch sizes from one to a hundred and twenty-eight. One detail I appreciated: they measure token throughput rather than per-request latency, because even with greedy decoding the outputs vary slightly between runs. The ablations cover batch size, draft length, chain versus tree, model size, and two oracle experiments that estimate the ceiling.

## Slide 9 — Speedup shrinks with batch size

Finding number one: the speedup shrinks as the server gets busy. EAGLE on Llama 8B with GSM8K gives one point seven three times at batch one, but only one point two one times at batch one hundred and twenty-eight. That's a thirty percent loss. And bigger models lose more: on ShareGPT, the 70B model loses about fourteen percent of its speedup by batch thirty-two, while the 8B model loses only four percent. The reason goes back to the refresher. Speculative decoding spends spare compute. At batch one the GPU is starved, so verifying extra tokens is nearly free. At batch one hundred and twenty-eight the GPU is already busy, and every speculative token you verify takes compute away from a real request.

## Slide 10 — Verification is the bottleneck

Finding number two surprised me the most. Most research effort goes into building better drafters. But when you profile where the time goes, the target model's verification pass takes between forty-two and ninety-five percent of total execution time. Drafting with n-gram costs less than two percent. Even EAGLE drafting is only twelve to twenty percent at batch one. Sampling is under two percent, and vLLM's own overhead is between three and twelve percent. So even a perfect, zero-cost drafter would barely change the picture at high batch sizes. The real cost is verifying tokens that end up being rejected.

## Slide 11 — Trees are a batch-one trick

Finding number three is about tree verification, where the drafter proposes several alternatives per position. At batch one, trees do help a little. On Qwen3 8B with GSM8K, a twenty-one-node tree reaches one point eight five times, versus one point six five for a simple chain. But look at the right panel. The wide tree keeps about thirty percent more tokens per step, while its acceptance rate falls from about forty-one percent to under ten percent. Nine out of ten verified tokens get thrown away. At batch sixty-four that waste can no longer hide in idle compute, and the twenty-one-node tree becomes slower than no speculation at all, on every workload. The plain chain stays above one. My takeaway: trees are a latency feature for lightly loaded servers, not a throughput feature.

## Slide 12 — No single winner, and a high ceiling

Finding number four: no drafter wins everywhere, and sometimes the simplest one wins. On InstructCoder, a code-editing dataset, the output repeats much of the input. The free n-gram lookup accepts about seven point three tokens per step on average, compared with four point two for EAGLE. The authors show that n-gram's advantage grows with the overlap between prompt and output, and above a certain overlap it wins at every batch size. Acceptance is also very uneven within a single request: n-gram is bursty, with occasional runs of fifteen accepted tokens and then nothing, while EAGLE is steadier. On the right is finding number five, the ceiling. If an oracle told us the perfect draft length at every step, we'd get about two point seven five times instead of two point one with the best fixed setting. And an oracle that picks n-gram or EAGLE at each position gives up to two point two times more on top of the best single strategy. These are upper bounds, not real systems, but they point at the next opportunity: adaptive, per-step decisions.

## Slide 13 — More from the vLLM study

A few more details from the Berkeley paper that matter for deployment. First, the drafter cost ratio decides whether separate draft models make sense. For the 70B target the small drafter costs about twelve and a half percent of a target pass, and it's the best method. For the 8B target it costs about thirty-seven percent, and it's the worst. Second, reasoning workloads are a sweet spot: on Qwen3 8B in thinking mode, EAGLE-3 reaches between one point six and one point eight times on AIME and GPQA. Third, MTP heads fade with distance. On GLM-4.5-Air, acceptance drops from zero point nine two at the first position to zero point three eight at the third, because the released head was trained mainly for the next token. And fourth, memory is mostly fine. N-gram adds nothing, and EAGLE-3 adds about five percent static memory, but a separate draft model raises KV cache per token by about one point eight times, and that memory comes straight out of your batch size.

## Slide 14 — Beyond text

Now let's look at the frontier, starting with the multimodal survey. Its first half is a broad survey of speculative decoding beyond text. For vision-language models, researchers reuse or prune visual tokens so the drafter stays cheap. For video, they use sparse attention and pipelining. For vision-language-action models, like robots and self-driving systems, they relax the acceptance rule, because a slightly different steering angle is usually fine. Speech recognition and text-to-speech use tolerance-based acceptance as well, and autoregressive image generators use relaxed acceptance in latent space. The key insight is that for continuous outputs, exact equality is often the wrong test, and close enough is the natural one. The second half of the paper is a benchmark on Qwen vision-language models across six tasks.

## Slide 15 — Multimodal results

Here are the results, and the leaderboard is clear. On Qwen3.6 27B, the sequential EAGLE-3 drafter gives one point six nine times. The MTP head gives one point six four. The block-parallel DSpark gives two point zero four. And the diffusion drafter DFlash wins every task, with about four point four accepted tokens per step and two point six times end-to-end speedup. On the smaller native vision-language models the gap is even more dramatic: EAGLE-3 is actually slower than plain decoding, at zero point seven one and zero point eight eight times, while DFlash gets roughly two times. So level-two, block-parallel drafters are the current state of the art.

## Slide 16 — Why only partially ready

But the authors call multimodal speculative decoding only "partially ready", and these two charts explain why. On the left, encoding and conditioning on the image takes about twenty-six milliseconds, while drafting a whole block takes only one to two milliseconds. The image prefill dominates. On the right, at high resolution the gain disappears: four-K images give between zero point nine six and one point one times, and eight-K images are actually slower, even though acceptance stays healthy. One ablation I liked: hiding the visual KV entries from the drafter reduces acceptance by only about four percent, so the drafter relies mostly on text context. That suggests compressing visual input to the drafter would be cheap. The authors are also honest that model, drafter checkpoint and backend all change together, so we should trust the direction more than the exact numbers.

## Slide 17 — How strict should the check be?

Our third paper asks a provocative question: what if we stop being lossless? The Graphcore and Imperial team puts all training-free relaxed methods into one framework. Relaxation means replacing the target distribution with a looser one at up to three points: the accept-or-reject test, the resampling after a rejection, and the bonus token. They group methods into three families. KL-bounded methods, like CACTUS and mentored decoding, maximize acceptance while staying within a divergence budget from the target. Threshold or defer methods, like fuzzy speculative decoding and speculative cascades, accept when the two models are close enough. And mix or contrast methods, like ensembles and speculative contrastive decoding, blend the two distributions. The last of these is the only one trying to improve quality rather than speed. The testbed is hard reasoning in thinking mode: AIME, GPQA Diamond and LiveCodeBench, with Qwen and Llama drafter-target pairs and about a thousand queries per data point.

## Slide 18 — Relaxed results

The upside is real. With CACTUS at a draft length of twenty, accepted tokens per step roughly double: from about eight to fifteen with a strong drafter, and from about five to ten with an MTP head. But the bill arrives elsewhere. First, you lose the free lunch on quality. Strict speculative decoding never needs re-evaluation, but relaxed decoding does, for each task, and the same relaxation setting behaves very differently on AIME and GPQA. Second, weak and MTP drafters can make the model ramble, producing longer, repetitive answers that can be slower overall even though tokens per second go up. Third, many relaxed methods assume the drafter is itself a good language model, which conflicts with the industry's move towards lightweight MTP heads. And finally, simply tuning the draft length moved the speedup about as much as relaxation did, and that's lossless. So tune k first.

## Slide 19 — The serving layer

The fourth paper takes us one layer down, to the KV cache that every speedup depends on. The Melbourne survey organizes system-aware KV optimization along three axes. Temporal: when is KV computed or used? That covers KV-aware scheduling, overlapping compute with data movement, and splitting prefill and decode onto different hardware. Spatial: where does KV live? That means tiers across GPU, CPU and SSD, keeping the important entries on the GPU, and moving KV between nodes. And structural: how is KV stored? That covers paged allocation and prefix reuse, quantization, low-rank compression, and eviction methods like H2O and SnapKV. The survey also notes that energy and tail latency are under-reported, and that compression is rarely combined with other techniques. It doesn't discuss speculative decoding directly, and I think that's a missed connection, because every draft token, tree branch and draft model adds KV cache that this layer has to absorb.

## Slide 20 — Cheat-sheet

So what would I actually deploy? Here's my cheat-sheet. If your outputs echo your inputs, as in code edits, RAG answers that quote sources, or rewriting, start with n-gram lookup. It's free. If you're serving at low concurrency and care about latency, as in chat, single users or on-device, use EAGLE-3 or an MTP head with a chain, and maybe try small trees. If you have a very large target with a matching tiny model, a separate draft model works well. If you're running high-throughput batch serving, keep chains short, or turn speculation off and measure. For vision-language serving, look at level-two block drafters like DFlash, but profile your image prefill first. And only use relaxed acceptance if you own your evaluation pipeline, because it's no longer lossless. Above all, measure your batch size first. It's the single best predictor of whether speculative decoding will help.

## Slide 21 — My two cents

Here's my own take. What these papers get right: the Berkeley study measures inside the engine people actually deploy, at the batch sizes they actually run, and its finding that verification dominates should redirect research from building better guessers to checking more cleverly. The relaxed decoding paper's habit of reporting end-to-end speedup, including longer answers, is a model of honest benchmarking. Where I'm skeptical: the oracle gains assume perfect foresight, and a real router will make mistakes and costs time itself. The multimodal benchmark changes several factors at once. The relaxed speedups are mostly modelled rather than measured on the wall clock. And the KV survey leaves out speculative decoding, even though KV schedulers that know about draft tokens seem like an obvious opportunity.

## Slide 22 — Where the field is heading

Looking ahead, I see the field moving from "one drafter, fixed draft length, always on" towards a runtime policy. That means verifying less, by only checking tokens likely to be accepted. It means adaptive per-step routing between n-gram, EAGLE, MTP, or no speculation, with the draft length chosen online. It means better MTP heads trained for positions beyond the next token, and diffusion drafters that model dependencies inside the block. For multimodal models, it means compressing the visual context for the drafter, and falling back to plain decoding at high resolution. On the serving side, it means KV schedulers that understand draft tokens, with energy and tail latency reported properly. And relaxed decoding becomes a product decision, backed by task-specific evaluation.

That's the story. To summarize in one sentence: speculative decoding is real performance at low load and on the right workloads, but much of the headline speedup is an illusion once a server is busy, and the next gains will come from adaptive policies and smarter verification rather than better drafters alone.
